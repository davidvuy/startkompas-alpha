# EU — unsolicited commercial communication (shared layer)

**This is a shared layer, not a country — and unlike the GDPR layer, it does not hold identically.** Art. 13 of Directive 2002/58/EC sets the frame for cold email, cold calls, faxes and automated calls. It is a **directive**, and one sentence in it hands the member states a choice. All four packs took that choice differently, so **the same first email is lawful in one of these countries and a criminal offence in another.**

Read this together with the relevant national pack. Neither replaces the other.

**This layer is preparation, not advice.** It records what the directive and the four national texts state, with dates. A directive binds member states, not businesses — what applies to a founder is their own country's implementing law, and the national rows below are the operative ones.

**Why this layer exists at all.** Every pilot this skill supports begins by contacting someone who did not ask to be contacted. That makes this the first rule a founder meets, before any question of contracts, invoicing or data protection, and it is the one the four packs disagree about most sharply.

## The sentence that makes the four packs disagree — article 13(5)

Art. 13(1) is the famous one: automated calling systems, faxes **or electronic mail** for direct marketing *"may be allowed only in respect of subscribers or users who have given their prior consent."*

Then art. 13(5) narrows who that protects:

> Paragraphs 1 and 3 shall apply to subscribers who are natural persons. Member States shall also ensure, in the framework of Community law and applicable national legislation, that the legitimate interests of subscribers other than natural persons with regard to unsolicited communications are sufficiently protected.

**So the opt-in is mandatory only for natural persons.** For everyone else — companies, and in some readings a professional at a work address — each member state had to decide for itself what *"sufficiently protected"* means. Four member states, four answers:

| Pack | What it did with the art. 13(5) discretion | Where the rule lives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Netherlands** | extended the opt-in to legal persons **outright**, then carved out addresses published for the purpose | telecoms law — art. 11.7 `Telecommunicatiewet` |
| **Germany** | extended it to **every addressee**, with no business carve-out at all | **competition** law — §7 `UWG` |
| **Belgium** | extended it to every addressee, then exempted **impersonal** company addresses by royal decree | **economic** law — art. XII.13 `WER` |
| **France** | left the opt-in at natural persons and lets B2B run on **GDPR legitimate interest** | telecoms law plus the CNIL's doctrine |

**Read the country, not the continent.** A founder who learns the rule in one of these four and applies it in another will be wrong in a way that is cheap to avoid and expensive to discover.

Two more paragraphs of art. 13 matter and are the same everywhere:

- **Art. 13(2) — the existing-customer exception.** Where a person obtains **from its customers** their email contact details *"in the context of the sale of a product or a service"*, it may use them for direct marketing of **its own similar** products or services, provided customers are *"clearly and distinctly"* given the chance to object, **free of charge and in an easy manner**, both **at the time of collection** and **on the occasion of each message**. All four national texts reproduce this; the table further down compares how.
- **Art. 13(4) — four things prohibited in any event**: disguising or concealing the sender's identity; contravening art. 6 of Directive 2000/31/EC; having **no valid address** for a request that the messages cease; and encouraging recipients to visit sites that contravene that article.

## The same cold email, four answers

The question a founder actually asks: *may I email a company I have never dealt with?*

| | **France** | **Netherlands** | **Belgium** | **Germany** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cold **email** to a named professional (`anna.jansen@firma.nl`) | **Yes** — legitimate interest, no consent, where the subject relates to their profession | **No** without consent — unless that address was **published for receiving** such messages | **No** without consent — the exemption covers only **impersonal** addresses | **No** without prior **express** consent. No business carve-out exists |
| Cold **email** to a generic address (`info@`, `contact@`) | **Yes** — the CNIL puts these outside the rules as data of legal persons | only on the same published-for-the-purpose test | **Yes** — the royal decree exempts impersonal addresses of legal persons | **No exception appears** — §7(2) Nr. 2 says `Adressaten` and draws no line at an impersonal address. Whether German practice draws one anyway is **[not researched]** |
| Cold **telephone** call to a business | **Yes** — legitimate interest, same test as email | the opt-in in art. 11.7(2) reaches **natural persons only**, so a call to a company is outside it | **Opt-out, plus a lookup before every call.** Art. VI.110 §2 permits a human call unless the subscriber has manifestly objected — but art. VI.112 §1 forbids calling any number on the **`Bel me niet meer!`** list and requires the caller to **check it beforehand, every time**. Automated calls and faxes need consent | **Yes, on a lower standard** — §7(2) Nr. 1 needs only **presumed** consent from a non-consumer |
| Who comes after you | the **CNIL** | the **ACM**, not the data-protection authority | **a criminal sanction** under art. XV.120. By what route it is pursued, and whether Book XV's administrative settlement comes first, is **[not researched]** | **a competitor**, by injunction. For email there is no administrative fine |
| Burden of proof | the controller's, under the GDPR | explicit: the sender *"kan aantonen"* | explicit: art. XII.13 §4 puts it on the sender | the advertiser's, as the party relying on consent |

**The two ends of that table are worth stating plainly.** In France the ordinary first email to a business contact is lawful on legitimate interest, with notice and an opt-out. In Germany the same email is unlawful without prior express consent, and in Belgium it is a **criminal offence** — art. XV.120 says so, though how a prosecution or settlement actually proceeds is not researched here. Nothing about the message changes; only the country does.

**Belgium splits by channel too, and the split runs the other way from Germany's.** An email needs consent and is criminal at `niveau 3`; a human telephone call is opt-out at `niveau 2` — but carries a duty to check a register before every call that no other pack has. So in Belgium the cheaper channel is the telephone, and the cheaper channel is the one with the procedural trap.

**And the asymmetry inside Germany is the opposite of the French one.** Germany treats a **cold call** to a business more leniently than a cold email — presumed consent suffices for the call, express consent is required for the email. France has no such split. A founder who switches channel to be safer may be switching to the stricter one.

## What each national text says

### Germany — §7 UWG, and why the sanction is a competitor rather than a regulator

`Gesetz gegen den unlauteren Wettbewerb`, §7 `Unzumutbare Belästigungen`:

- **§7(2) Nr. 2** — an unreasonable nuisance is **always** to be assumed in advertising using an automatic calling machine, a fax, or **`elektronischer Post`**, *"ohne dass eine vorherige ausdrückliche Einwilligung des Adressaten vorliegt"* — without the **prior express consent of the addressee**. The word is `Adressaten`, not `Verbraucher`: **there is no B2B exception.**
- **§7(2) Nr. 1** — for a **telephone call**, the standard splits: a `Verbraucher` needs prior **express** consent, *"gegenüber einem sonstigen Marktteilnehmer"* — any other market participant — only *"zumindest mutmaßliche Einwilligung"*, **at least presumed consent**.
- **§7(2) Nr. 3** — also always a nuisance: concealing the identity of the sender on whose behalf the message is sent; breaching §6(1) `Digitale-Dienste-Gesetz`, or inviting the recipient to a website that does; and having **no valid address** the recipient can use to demand the messages stop at no more than basic transmission cost.
- **§7(3)** — the existing-customer exception, in **four numbered points that must all hold**: the trader obtained the email address **from the customer in connection with the sale** of a product or service; uses it for direct advertising of **its own similar** goods or services; the customer **has not objected**; and the customer is told **clearly and plainly, at collection and at every use**, that they may object at any time at no more than basic transmission cost.

**Then the part that decides what actually happens to you.** §20 `Bußgeldvorschriften` makes it an administrative offence to advertise *"mit einem Telefonanruf oder unter Verwendung einer automatischen Anrufmaschine gegenüber einem Verbraucher"* without prior express consent — **a telephone call or automatic calling machine, to a consumer**, fined up to **€300,000**, with the **Bundesnetzagentur** as the competent authority. **Email is not in that list.**

So cold email in Germany is unlawful but carries no administrative fine. The enforcement route is **§8**: a claim for removal and an injunction, held by *"jedem Mitbewerber, der Waren oder Dienstleistungen in nicht unerheblichem Maße und nicht nur gelegentlich vertreibt oder nachfragt"* — **any competitor** trading to a not insignificant extent and not merely occasionally — by registered trade associations, by qualified consumer associations, and by the **chambers of industry and commerce**. A founder's practical risk in Germany is therefore a letter from a competitor, not a decision from an authority.

**And one duty a founder will not expect.** §7a, `Einwilligung in Telefonwerbung`: whoever advertises **by telephone to a consumer** must document the prior express consent *"zum Zeitpunkt der Erteilung in angemessener Form"* and keep the proof for **five years** from the consent and after **each use** of it, producing it to the Bundesnetzagentur **without delay** on request. Failing to document or keep it is its own offence under §20(1) Nr. 2, up to **€50,000**.

**Compare France, which requires the same proof for three years** — see the retention row in the comparison below. Same obligation, two numbers, and the stricter one is not where a founder would guess.

### Netherlands — art. 11.7 Telecommunicatiewet, which covers companies

Read in the version `Geldend van 15-08-2026`:

- **Art. 11.7(1)** — using automatic calling and communication systems without human intervention, faxes and **`elektronische berichten`** to convey unsolicited communication for commercial, idealistic or charitable purposes is **prohibited**, *"tenzij de verzender kan aantonen dat de desbetreffende eindgebruiker daarvoor voorafgaand toestemming heeft verleend"* — unless the sender **can demonstrate** prior consent from the `eindgebruiker`.
- **And `eindgebruiker` is defined in art. 1.1 as a `natuurlijke persoon of rechtspersoon`.** That is the whole point: the Dutch opt-in for email **covers companies**, which art. 13(5) did not require. The burden of proof is written into the provision rather than inferred.
- **Art. 11.7(2)** extends the prohibition to **other** means — in practice the human-made telephone call — but *"aan natuurlijke personen"* only, with a clear and explicit free objection route in **every** message. A cold call to a company therefore falls outside this article.
- **Art. 11.7(3)** is the business carve-out, and it is narrow. No prior consent is needed for unsolicited communication to a **legal person, or a natural person acting in the exercise of their profession or business**, if either:
  - **(a)** the sender uses electronic contact details that the end user has **designated and made public for receiving** such unsolicited communication, *"en deze worden gebruikt in overeenstemming met de door de eindgebruiker aan de contactgegevens verbonden doeleinden"* — and uses them in accordance with the purposes the end user attached to them; or
  - **(b)** the end user is established **outside the European Economic Area** and the rules of that country are complied with.
- **Art. 11.7(4)** is the existing-customer exception: details obtained **from customers** when selling a product or service, used for the sender's **own similar** products or services, with a clear and explicit free and easy objection route **at collection** and, where the subscriber did not object then, **in every message sent**.
- **Art. 20.7** is a transitional rule worth knowing if a list predates it: art. 11.7(4)(a) does not apply to contact details collected **before** the entry into force of the `wet van 10 februari 2021` that introduced the opt-in system for communication to natural persons.

**The crux of (a) is the purpose, not the publication.** A company publishing `info@` on its website has published a contact address; whether it designated it *for receiving unsolicited advertising* is a different question, and the article conditions the exemption on the second. Treat a published address as inside (a) only where the page says so.

**And the authority is not the one a founder expects.** Art. 15.1(2) `Tw` gives the **Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens** only arts. 11.3a and 11.5b plus certain security-breach matters; art. 15.1(3) gives the **Autoriteit Consument en Markt** supervision of every other provision of the act, and art. 15.2(2) gives the ACM the `last onder bestuursdwang` to enforce it. **Art. 11.7 is the ACM's**, not the data-protection authority's.

Source · [Telecommunicatiewet, arts. 1.1, 11.7, 15.1, 15.2 and 20.7](https://wetten.overheid.nl/BWBR0009950) · *primary legislation, read on the official portal in the version `Geldend van 15-08-2026`* · **[checked 2026-11-12]**

### Belgium — art. XII.13 WER, where this is a criminal offence

- **Art. XII.13 §1** `Wetboek van economisch recht` / `Code de droit économique`: using electronic mail for advertising purposes is **prohibited** *"sans le consentement préalable, libre, spécifique et informé du destinataire des messages"* — without the prior, free, specific and informed consent of the **addressee**. The King may provide exceptions. Inserted by `L 2013-12-15/51`, in force **2014-05-31**.
- **Art. XII.13 §2**: with **every** advertising email the provider must give clear and comprehensible information about the right to object for the future, and must **indicate and make available an appropriate means of exercising that right effectively by electronic means**.
- **Art. XII.13 §3** prohibits using a third party's address or identity, falsifying or masking information identifying the message's origin or transmission path, and encouraging the recipient to visit sites infringing art. XII.12.
- **Art. XII.13 §4**: *"La preuve du caractère sollicité des publicités par courrier électronique incombe au prestataire"* — **the burden of proving that the advertising was solicited falls on the sender.**

**The exceptions are in a royal decree that names a repealed law, and that is fine.** `Arrêté royal du 4 avril 2003 visant à réglementer l'envoi de publicités par courrier électronique` exempts a provider from seeking prior consent:

- **1°** from **its clients, natural or legal persons**, where all of the following hold: it obtained their electronic contact details **directly** in the context of the sale of a product or service and in compliance with privacy law; it uses them to advertise **exclusively analogous products or services that it itself supplies**; and it gives the client, **at the moment the details are collected**, the ability to object *"sans frais et de manière simple"*.
- **2°** from **legal persons, if the electronic contact details used are `impersonnelles`** — impersonal. This is Belgium's answer to art. 13(5), and it is the only one of the four that turns on the **shape of the address** rather than on who holds it or why you are writing.

The decree executes art. 14 §1 of the `loi du 11 mars 2003`, which art. 5 of `L 2013-12-15/51` **repealed**. It still operates, and the reason is in the same law: **art. 6** provides that existing laws or royal decrees referring to the repealed acts *"sont présumées faire référence aux dispositions équivalentes du Code de droit économique"*. So the 2003 decree's reference to art. 14 §1 is presumed to be a reference to art. XII.13 §1. A reader who checks the decree alone sees a dead cross-reference and concludes the exemptions lapsed; they did not.

**Art. 2 of the decree adds a duty none of the other three has.** Anyone may tell a provider, free and without giving reasons, that they no longer wish to receive its advertising email. The provider must then, within a reasonable time, **send an acknowledgement of receipt by email** confirming the request was registered, take the measures needed to respect it, and **keep up-to-date lists** of the people who have notified it. An unsubscribe link that silently suppresses the address does not discharge the first of those.

**And the penalty is criminal.** Art. XV.120 WER: those who send advertising by email in breach of art. XII.13 are punished with a `sanction du niveau 3`; art. XV.121 raises it to `niveau 4` for a breach committed **in bad faith**. Art. XV.70 §1 defines the scale:

| Level | Criminal fine | Or, if higher |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **niveau 3** — breach of art. XII.13 | €26 to **€25,000** | **6 %** of total annual turnover of the last closed financial year |
| **niveau 4** — the same in bad faith | €26 to **€50,000** | **6 %** of total annual turnover |

**Two cautions on those figures, and the first one is not hypothetical.**

**The surcharge exists and art. XV.70 does not mention it.** `Wet van 5 maart 1952 betreffende de opdécimes op de strafrechtelijke geldboeten` art. 1 raises *"het bedrag der strafrechtelijke geldboeten uitgesproken door de hoven en rechtbanken krachtens het Strafwetboek en de bijzondere, zelfs van na deze wet daterende wetten en reglementen"* — fines under the Penal Code **and under special laws, including later ones** — *"zonder dat die verhoging het juridisch karakter van die boeten wijzigt"*. The WER is such a special law. Since **2026-09-01** the surcharge is **`2,5 opdecimes`**, after two amendments in a year: `W 2025-12-19/18` in force 2026-02-01 and `W 2026-03-16/16` in force 2026-09-01. The article's own note records how far it has moved before — 190 opdecimes in 1952, rising to 1490 by 1994.

**So the honest statement is that €25,000 may not be the ceiling, and the pack does not convert the figure.** Art. 1's new third paragraph sends special laws *"die geen strafniveau bepalen"* to art. 78, § 1, second paragraph of the `Strafwetboek van 29 februari 2024` — and art. XV.70 **does** set levels, so that paragraph does not obviously reach it. Whether the surcharge then applies on top of a `niveau 3` fine, and what `2,5 opdecimes` comes to, is a question for a Belgian lawyer. **Give a founder the article and the fact that a surcharge regime exists, never a multiplied number.**

**And Belgian criminal law is a moving target right now.** The `wet van 29 februari 2024` introducing Book II of the Penal Code has its articles 78 onward **repealed** by `W 2026-03-16/08`, art. 126, in force **2026-09-01** — the same date as the surcharge change and as the restatement of the data-protection act's penalties that [belgium-data-protection.md](belgium-data-protection.md) records. Any secondary source on a Belgian criminal penalty written before September 2026 should be assumed stale.

**The second caution: this `niveau` scale is the WER's own**, defined in art. XV.70 — it is **not** the `straf van niveau 1` of the new Penal Code that [belgium-data-protection.md](belgium-data-protection.md) records for art. 222 of the data-protection act. Same word, different scales, and conflating them gets the number wrong by an order of magnitude.

Source · [`Wet van 5 maart 1952 betreffende de opdécimes op de strafrechtelijke geldboeten`](https://www.ejustice.just.fgov.be/eli/wet/1952/03/05/1952030505/justel) · *primary legislation, read on Justel's consolidated text, `tekstbijwerking tot 01-04-2026`* · **[checked 2026-11-12]**

So Belgium is the **second** place in these packs where ordinary pilot activity is criminal rather than administrative — the first being processing without a lawful basis under art. 222. Both are in the same country, and neither is in the other three.

### Belgium again — the telephone rule is a different article, a different penalty, and a duty to look something up

Art. XII.13 governs **email**. Telephone direct marketing sits in Book VI, `HOOFDSTUK 3. — Ongewenste communicaties`, and answers differently:

| What | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| **Automated calling systems without human intervention, and faxes** | **Prohibited** without the *"voorafgaande, vrije, specifieke en geïnformeerde toestemming van de geadresseerde"* — art. VI.110 §1. Consent is withdrawable at any time, without a reason and at no cost, and **the burden of proving the communication was requested rests on the sender** |
| **A human telephone call** | **Opt-out.** Art. VI.110 §2, as replaced with effect from 2024-02-29: *"Andere telefonische communicatie met het oog op direct marketing is toegestaan voor zover de abonnee of de gebruiker zich hiertegen niet kennelijk heeft verzet"* — permitted so long as the subscriber or user **has not manifestly objected**, and subject to arts. VI.111 to VI.115 |
| **But first, the list** | Art. VI.112 §1: a direct-marketing call to a number on the **`Bel me niet meer!`** list is prohibited, and *"[v]oor elke telefoonoproep om redenen van direct marketing gaat de oproeper voorafgaandelijk na of het desbetreffende nummer niet is opgenomen"* — **before every such call the caller checks whether the number is on it.** The exception in §2 is narrow: subscribers who gave **that** caller express permission to use their data for the purpose |
| **Identity** | Art. VI.110 §4: concealing the identity of the undertaking on whose behalf the communication takes place is prohibited |
| **Cost of objecting** | Art. VI.110 §3: nothing may be charged to the addressee for exercising the right to object |
| **Penalty** | Art. XV.83, 16°: breaching arts. VI.110 to VI.115 is a **`sanctie van niveau 2`** — by art. XV.70 §1, €26 to **€10,000**, or **4 %** of total annual turnover if that is higher. Lower than the `niveau 3` for email |

**This is the only duty in these four packs that is a lookup rather than a basis.** France, Germany and the Netherlands tell a founder what they need before calling; Belgium also tells them to consult a register **before each call**, and art. VI.114 has the list made available to businesses by a non-profit recognised by the King. A founder working a call list in Belgium either has that check in their process or is in breach of it on every call.

**One scope question, left open rather than assumed.** The articles say `abonnee` and `gebruiker`, not `consument`, and the chapter sits in the same Title as the expressly business-to-business art. VI.109/8 — so nothing on the face of them limits the rule to consumers. Whether a **business** number is covered in practice is a question about Book VI's scope that was not researched, and the open row below says so.

### France — the one that permits the first email

France left the opt-in where art. 13(5) put it, at natural persons, and the CNIL holds that **B2B prospecting may rest on legitimate interest** where the subject of the approach relates to the recipient's profession — with information and a simple free objection route at collection and in every message. Generic company addresses are outside the rules entirely as data of legal persons. The telephone rule for **consumers** changed to opt-in on **2026-08-11**, with consent valid one year and its proof kept **three** years.

The whole of it, with the CNIL's own examples and its October 2026 position on **guessing** a professional address from a name, is in [france-data-protection.md](france-data-protection.md). It is `Authority` rather than `Primary`: Légifrance serves a bot-protection challenge, so art. L34-5 `CPCE` has not been read in primary form — see that annex for what that costs.

## The existing-customer exception, compared

All four reproduce art. 13(2), and a founder's second campaign — to people who have already bought — is the one that turns on it.

| | France | Netherlands | Belgium | Germany |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Where | the CNIL's reading of the consumer rule | art. 11.7(4) `Tw` | art. 1, 1° of the 2003 royal decree | §7(3) `UWG` |
| Details obtained | from the customer, on a sale | from customers, on a sale | **directly**, on a sale, and in compliance with privacy law | from the customer, in connection with a sale |
| What you may advertise | similar products or services of the same company | own similar products or services | **exclusively** analogous products or services it supplies itself | own similar goods or services |
| Objection offered | at collection and in every message | at collection and, absent objection then, in every message | **at collection** — the decree does not add "and in every message" here | at collection **and at every use** |
| Covers a business customer? | the exception is framed for consumers; B2B needs no exception | yes — (4) is not limited to natural persons | **yes, expressly** — *"personnes physiques ou morales"* | yes — `Kunde`, not `Verbraucher` |
| The trap | an online account without a purchase does **not** make someone a client | — | — | all four numbered points must hold together |

**The Belgian column is the odd one.** Its decree conditions the exemption on an objection route offered at collection, where the directive and the other three also require one in each message. Art. XII.13 §2 supplies that duty separately and for **every** advertising email, so the result is the same — but a reader comparing only the exception clauses would conclude Belgium is laxer, and it is not.
