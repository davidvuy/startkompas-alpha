# EU — small-enterprise VAT scheme

**This is a shared layer, not a country.** Council Directive (EU) 2020/285 sets the frame every EU member state's small-business VAT exemption sits inside. The national figures differ; the ceiling on them and the cross-border rule do not.

Read this together with the relevant national pack. Neither replaces the other.

**This layer is preparation, not advice.** It records what the directive states, with dates. A directive binds member states, not businesses directly — what applies to a founder is their own country's implementing law, which only an accountant in that country can confirm.

## The two thresholds the directive sets

Source: [Council Directive (EU) 2020/285, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32020L0285) · *primary EU legislation* · **[checked 2026-11-10]**

| | Figure | Provision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ceiling on a national threshold** — the most a member state may set | **€85,000** or the national-currency equivalent | art. 284(1): *"shall be no higher than EUR 85 000"* |
| **Union annual turnover** — the cap for the cross-border exemption | **€100,000** | art. 284(2)(a): *"the Union annual turnover of that taxable person does not exceed EUR 100 000"* |

Member states had to adopt the provisions by **2024-12-31** and apply them from **2025-01-01** (art. 3(1)).

## What this means in practice

**The national figure is a choice within a ceiling.** €85,000 is not anybody's threshold — it is the maximum a member state is allowed to set. That is why the four national packs show €20,000 (NL), €25,000 (BE and DE's previous-year figure), €37,500 (FR services) and €85,000 (FR sales, which sits exactly at the ceiling). A founder asking "what is the EU threshold" is asking a question with no answer: there is a ceiling, and then there is their country's figure.

**The cross-border exemption has two conditions, not one.** Union annual turnover must stay under €100,000, **and** the business must stay under the national threshold of every member state where it applies the scheme. Meeting one is not enough. The Dutch EU-KOR page states both conditions explicitly, which is consistent with the directive.

**€100,000 is an EU figure, not a national one.** Where a national pack mentions a €100,000 cross-border threshold, it is this provision. Do not treat it as that country's own invention, and do not confuse it with Germany's €100,000 **current-year domestic** figure under §19 UStG — which is an unrelated number that happens to coincide. That coincidence is a live trap: the German figure is domestic and current-year, the directive's is Union-wide and prior-year.

## Using the exemption in another member state — the mechanics

All from the same directive, articles 284 to 284e · **[checked 2026-11-10]**

**Prior notification, and an `EX` number.** A small enterprise must give **prior notification to its member state of establishment** before using the exemption elsewhere. That state then issues an **individual identification number with the suffix `EX`**. Identification happens **no later than 35 working days** after notification, which member states may extend where they suspect tax evasion.

So the cross-border exemption is not something you simply apply: it has an application, a waiting period and its own number.

**A quarterly return.** For **each calendar quarter**, report to the member state of establishment:

- the total value of supplies in your own member state, **or `0`**
- the total value of supplies in **each** other member state, **or `0`**

Due **within one month of the end of the quarter**. The explicit "or 0" matters: a nil quarter still needs filing.

This is a real recurring obligation that the domestic exemption does not have. A founder who takes the cross-border exemption trades quarterly VAT returns for quarterly turnover returns — do not present it as pure simplification.

**How the two turnover figures are defined.**

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Annual turnover **in the member state** | *"the total annual value of supplies of goods and services, exclusive of VAT, made by a taxable person within that Member State during a calendar year"* |
| **Union** annual turnover | the same, *"within the territory of the Community during a calendar year"* |

Both are **exclusive of VAT** and measured **per calendar year**, not rolling.

## Crossing the Union threshold mid-year

**The exemption stops immediately, not at year end.** Where the €100,000 Union threshold is exceeded during a calendar year, the exemption granted to a taxable person **not established** in the member state granting it *"shall cease to apply as of that time"*.

And there is a **15 working day** notification deadline when the Union threshold is breached — separate from, and much shorter than, the quarterly return.

Note what this does **not** settle: it governs the **cross-border** exemption for a non-established person. It does not tell you what happens to a founder's **domestic** exemption when a domestic threshold is crossed mid-year. That is a national question, and the national packs answer it very differently:

| Pack | What happens on a mid-year domestic crossing |
| --- | --- |
| **France** | Stated on the face of the official page. Crossing the `seuil` (N-1) means VAT from 1 January of the following year; crossing the `seuil majoré` (N) ends the franchise *"dès le 1er jour du dépassement"* — see [france.md](france.md) §2 |
| **Germany** | §19 UStG was read directly and is **silent**. The pack records the claims circulating about it as having no identified statutory basis — see [germany.md](germany.md) §2 |
| **Netherlands** | **The exemption ends immediately.** The Belastingdienst draws the distinction itself: a voluntary exit from the KOR takes effect from the first day of the next quarter, but crossing €20,000 in a calendar year means *"u zich meteen afmelden ... Uw afmelding gaat dan direct in"* — see [netherlands.md](netherlands.md) §2 |
| **Belgium** | **A 10 % tolerance, and it is the only one.** Art. 56decies §1: up to ten per cent over the threshold the exemption survives to the end of the calendar year; beyond that it stops at once — and the following calendar year is barred. See [belgium.md](belgium.md) §2 |

So "what happens if I go over mid-year" now has a clear answer in three of the four packs, and they do **not** all agree. **The French `seuil majoré` and the Dutch KOR both end the exemption on the day, not at year end** — while **Belgium gives a 10 % tolerance band** before it ends, under art. 56decies §1 of its VAT Code ([belgium.md](belgium.md) §2, primary legislation read 2026-11-12). Germany's statute is silent. Three national answers to one question, which is the point of keeping them apart. Do not carry the French rule across a border; the French page's own history shows the rule can change even within one country, since it says the immediate cessation is *"et non plus au premier jour du mois du dépassement"*.

## What counts toward the turnover — article 288

Source: the same directive's amended **article 288** of the VAT Directive · **[checked 2026-11-10]**

**Included** in the reference turnover:

- the value of supplies of goods and services, *"in so far as they would be taxed were they supplied by a non-exempt taxable person"*
- transactions exempt **with** deductibility of input VAT under art. 110, 111 or 125(1)
- transactions exempt under art. 146 to 149 and art. 151, 152 and 153
- transactions exempt under art. 138, where that exemption applies
- **real estate transactions, the financial transactions in art. 135(1)(b)–(g), and insurance and reinsurance services — unless those are ancillary**

**Excluded:** *"Disposals of the tangible or intangible capital assets of a taxable person shall not be taken into account for the purposes of calculating the turnover."*

Two things a founder can get wrong here. First, **"exempt" does not mean "ignored"** — several categories of exempt transaction count toward the threshold, so a business can be pushed over it by turnover it charged no VAT on. Second, **selling equipment does not count**: disposing of capital assets is expressly left out, so a founder who sells a laptop or a domain name has not thereby moved closer to the threshold. That exclusion matches the one §19(2) UStG makes domestically for fixed assets.

The financial-and-real-estate limb turns on whether those transactions are **ancillary**, which is a judgment rather than a lookup and is not resolved here.
