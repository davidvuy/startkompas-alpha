# Netherlands — agreements and unfair terms (annex)

**This is an annex to [netherlands.md](netherlands.md) §4, not a pack of its own.** It exists because the Dutch pack reached this skill's 48 KB per-reference budget on 2026-11-13. §4 of the pack states the three questions in the order they have to be asked and the two rules that decide most cases; the article-by-article detail is here. Same split as [netherlands-copyright.md](netherlands-copyright.md) and [netherlands-data-protection.md](netherlands-data-protection.md).

**This annex is preparation, not advice.** It records what the articles of Boek 6 BW state, with dates, and marks the case law it has not read. Dutch case law is not published free of charge, and several of the questions a founder will actually ask turn on it. A consequential decision needs a Dutch `advocaat`.

Dutch law protects against unreasonable standard terms. The protection is calibrated partly by **how big you are**, which cuts in a founder's favour — and partly by **whether you use standard terms of your own**, which cuts the other way and is the half this section used to miss.

Sources
- [arts. 6:233, 6:235, 6:236 and 6:237 BW](https://wetten.overheid.nl/BWBR0005289) · *primary legislation, read on the official portal in the version `Geldend van 16-07-2026`* · **[checked 2026-11-11]**
- [arts. 6:225, 6:119a and 6:248 BW](https://wetten.overheid.nl/BWBR0005289) · *primary legislation, read on the official portal in the version `Geldend van 16-07-2026`* · **[checked 2026-11-13]**
- the `reflexwerking` doctrine, which is case law and not in the articles · *secondary legal commentary, consistent* · **[checked 2026-11-10]**

**Art. 6:233** makes a term in general conditions **voidable** on either of two grounds, quoted:

> *"indien het, gelet op de aard en de overige inhoud van de overeenkomst, de wijze waarop de voorwaarden zijn tot stand gekomen, de wederzijds kenbare belangen van partijen en de overige omstandigheden van het geval, onredelijk bezwarend is voor de wederpartij"*

> *"indien de gebruiker aan de wederpartij niet een redelijke mogelijkheid heeft geboden om van de algemene voorwaarden kennis te nemen"*

The second ground is independent of content: **failing to give the counterparty a real chance to read the terms is itself enough**, however reasonable the terms are.

| Provision | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **art. 6:233 BW** | Voidable on either ground above |
| **art. 6:235(1) BW** | Cannot be invoked by a party with **50 or more persons employed**, or by a legal entity that had **most recently published its annual accounts** at the time the agreement was concluded |
| **art. 6:236 / 6:237 BW** | The black list (deemed unreasonably onerous) and the grey list (presumed so) open with the same words: *“Bij een overeenkomst tussen een gebruiker en een wederpartij, **natuurlijk persoon, die niet handelt in de uitoefening van een beroep of bedrijf**”*. Read that test, not “consumer” |
| `reflexwerking` | Where a small entrepreneur's position closely resembles a consumer's, a term's appearance on the consumer black or grey list is relevant to the open test in 6:233 · *secondary* |

Reading them on 2026-11-11 worked by loading the page in a browser and slicing it to the article in the page itself, so only the article is ever extracted. The same method read art. 7:400 out of Boek 7, which is over a million characters. It is a tooling limit, not a source disagreement — and it was worth defeating: the reproduction had been right about arts. 233 and 235(1) and silent about art. 235(3), which is the paragraph that matters most to the reader of this section.

**For a solo founder this is usually favourable — but article 6:235 has two more paragraphs, and one of them can take it away.**

- **Art. 6:235(3):** the grounds in arts. 233 and 234 *"kan geen beroep worden gedaan door een partij die meermalen dezelfde of nagenoeg dezelfde algemene voorwaarden in haar overeenkomsten gebruikt"* — **a party that itself repeatedly uses the same or nearly the same general terms cannot invoke them.** Nothing about size. A founder who has their own standard terms and uses them with every client is inside this paragraph, and the protection the rest of this section describes is gone.
  - **It is about the founder's own repeated use, not about the two sets of terms resembling each other.** The resemblance the paragraph tests is between the founder's terms and the founder's *other* contracts — *"dezelfde of nagenoeg dezelfde algemene voorwaarden in haar overeenkomsten"*. Whether the counterparty's conditions look anything like the founder's does not enter it, and no paragraph of art. 6:235 asks that.
  - **And `meermalen` has no number in the statute.** Whether one set of terms used across a handful of pilots engages this paragraph is **a judgment for a Dutch lawyer**, and the pack cannot narrow it: the case law is **[not researched]**, not published free of charge. Say the exposure exists and say the threshold is unsettled. Do not estimate a count.
- **Art. 6:235(2):** the art. 233(a) ground may **also** be invoked by a party for whom the terms were used by an authorised representative, provided the counterparty repeatedly concludes contracts on the same or nearly the same terms.
- **Art. 6:235(4):** the limitation period of art. 3:52(1)(d) BW starts on the day after the term is invoked against you — so the clock runs from the moment the other side relies on the clause, not from signature.

Paragraph 3 is the one to raise before anything else in this section, because it is the only one that turns on the founder's **own** conduct rather than their size, and because §4's own advice — have your own terms, and give the counterparty a real chance to read them — is what puts a founder inside it. Do not tell a founder they are protected because they are small without raising it.

Subject to that, the size calibration still cuts in a small party's favour. You are a small party, so art. 6:235(1) does not exclude you — you *can* invoke 6:233 against a larger counterparty's standard terms, and `reflexwerking` may import the consumer lists in your favour. The failure to give you a real chance to read the terms is itself a ground.

The mirror image matters too: once a founder grows past 50 employees or publishes accounts, that protection disappears.

Use [../pilot-conditions.md](../pilot-conditions.md) §3 for the discussion sheet, and give the counterparty a genuine opportunity to read your own terms — under 6:233 that is not a formality.

### Before arguing about a clause, settle whose terms apply at all — art. 6:225

**Everything above this line assumes the clause is in the contract.** Art. 6:225(3) decides whether it is, and it can end the question without any argument about content:

> *"Verwijzen aanbod en aanvaarding naar verschillende algemene voorwaarden, dan komt aan de tweede verwijzing geen werking toe, wanneer daarbij niet tevens de toepasselijkheid van de in de eerste verwijzing aangegeven algemene voorwaarden uitdrukkelijk van de hand wordt gewezen."*

**The first reference wins unless the second one expressly rejects it.** Where offer and acceptance each point at different general terms, the second reference has **no effect** — unless it *also* expressly rejects the terms the first reference named. Merely declaring your own terms applicable is not a rejection; the statute requires the counterparty's terms to be *"uitdrukkelijk van de hand"* gewezen.

For a founder that makes the order of documents decisive, and it is usually recoverable from an inbox:

- **You quoted first, referring to your terms; their order confirmation referred to theirs without rejecting yours** → their terms have no effect, and the clause never applied.
- **Their purchase conditions came first and yours came second**, on a confirmation or an invoice → yours have no effect unless your own terms contain an express rejection clause. Whether yours does is worth checking once and fixing permanently.

**Two neighbouring paragraphs matter when the reply is not a clean acceptance.** Art. 6:225(1): an acceptance that deviates from the offer is *"een nieuw aanbod en een verwerping van het oorspronkelijke"* — a counter-offer and a rejection. Art. 6:225(2): where the reply deviates only on **subordinate** points it still counts as acceptance and the contract arises on the terms of that acceptance, **unless the offeror objects `onverwijld`** — without delay. So silence in the face of a small change is agreement to the change.

**What counts as a subordinate point, and how courts treat a boilerplate rejection clause, is case law and [not researched]** — the Dutch case law is not published free of charge. Say the rule and the order of events; do not predict the outcome.

### Payment terms have a statutory ceiling, and against a big client it is 30 days — art. 6:119a

**This is the one place in §4 where a founder has a hard statutory weapon rather than a voidability argument.** Art. 6:119a applies to a `handelsovereenkomst` — defined in lid 1 as an onerous contract obliging one or more parties to give or do something, concluded between natural persons acting in a profession or business, or legal persons. A freelancer invoicing a company is inside it.

| Provision | What it fixes |
| --- | --- |
| **lid 2** | With **no** agreed payment date, statutory commercial interest runs **by operation of law** from **30 days** after the day following receipt of the invoice — or of the performance where the invoice came first, or of acceptance where an inspection period was agreed |
| **lid 4** | An agreed **inspection or acceptance** period is at most **30 days** from receipt of the performance, unless expressly agreed longer **and** not *"kennelijk onbillijk"* toward the creditor |
| **lid 5** | Parties may agree a payment deadline of at most **60 days**, unless they expressly put a longer term in the contract **and** it is not manifestly unfair, judged on whether the debtor has objective reasons, the nature of the performance, and any significant departure from good commercial practice |
| **lid 6** | **A maximum of 30 days, with no escape**, where the debtor is a **large** legal person and the creditor is not — and *"een beding in een overeenkomst in strijd met de vorige zin is nietig"* |
| lid 3 | The amount interest is calculated on is increased each year by that year's interest — it compounds annually |
| lid 7 to 9 | No interest while the **creditor** is itself in default; none in so far as the delay is not attributable to the debtor; an agreed interest rate is treated as the statutory one |

**Lid 6 is worth stating exactly, because the shorthand "30 days for SMEs" is not what it says.** The test is on both sides and it runs on art. 2:397 lid 1 and 2 BW. **Art. 2:397 lid 1** sets three requirements, measured on two consecutive balance-sheet dates and without interruption thereafter on two consecutive balance-sheet dates:

| Requirement | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| a. assets, on the balance sheet with notes, at acquisition or production cost | **not more than €25,000,000** |
| b. net turnover for the financial year | **not more than €50,000,000** |
| c. average number of employees over the financial year | **fewer than 250** |

**Art. 2:397 lid 2** adds in the assets, turnover and employees of group companies that would have to be consolidated if the legal person had to prepare consolidated accounts — unless it applies art. 2:408.

Then art. 6:119a lid 6 asks:

- the **debtor** must be a legal person that did **not** meet at least two of those three. Failing two of three means exceeding two of those limits, which is the definition of a **large** company — art. 2:397 is the *medium-sized* regime, not the small one (art. 2:396 is small, with its own lower figures).
- the **creditor** must be a natural person acting in a profession or business, or a legal person, that **did** meet at least two of them over that period — which is every freelancer and nearly every SME.

Then 30 days is the ceiling and a longer term is **`nietig`** — void by operation of law, not voidable. Nothing has to be invoked, and no art. 6:235 bar touches it.

**So the cap reaches a genuinely large client, not merely a bigger one.** A client with €30m turnover and 100 employees still meets two of the three requirements, so it is medium-sized and lid 6 does not apply to it — the 60-day ceiling in lid 5 does. **"Not small" is the wrong gloss and this annex carried it for two hours on 2026-11-13; the statute says large.** Where a client's size is not obvious, its own published accounts at the KVK are the place to settle it.

**One figure here is secondary to that point but worth having**: art. 2:397's thresholds are the medium-sized limits for the *accounting* regime too, so a client that publishes an abbreviated balance sheet under lid 5 is telling you, on the public record, that it is not large.

### The fallback no bar reaches — art. 6:248(2)

Where art. 6:235 shuts the unfair-terms route, art. 6:248(2) is still open: *"Een tussen partijen als gevolg van de overeenkomst geldende regel is niet van toepassing, voor zover dit in de gegeven omstandigheden naar maatstaven van redelijkheid en billijkheid onaanvaardbaar zou zijn."*

**A rule that applies between the parties does not apply in so far as that would be unacceptable** by standards of reasonableness and fairness in the circumstances. It is the `derogerende werking` of good faith, it has no size test and no `meermalen` test, and the threshold — *"onaanvaardbaar"* — is deliberately higher than *"onredelijk bezwarend"*. Mention it as the door that is never closed, not as an easier route. **How courts apply it to standard terms is case law and [not researched].**

**Open:** the precise boundaries of `reflexwerking`, and what counts as `meermalen` under art. 6:235(3), are case law and **[not researched]** — the Dutch case law is not published free of charge. The articles themselves were read in primary form on 2026-11-11. Confirm either question with a Dutch lawyer before relying on it.
