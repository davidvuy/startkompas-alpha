## Why these cannot be generalized

Four neighbouring EU countries, one question — "must I send structured electronic invoices to my business customers?" — and four different answers as at 2026-11-10:

| | Must receive | Must issue (small founder) | How |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Belgium | since 2026-01-01 | **already, since 2026-01-01** | Peppol network |
| France | since 2026-09-01 | from **2027-09-01** | **accredited platform mandatory**, plus a separate e-reporting obligation |
| Germany | since 2025-01-01 | from **2028-01-01** | **an email address suffices to receive**; BMF grace period ends 2026-12-31 |
| Netherlands | no domestic mandate | announced for **2030-07-01**, not yet law | Peppol recommended, not mandated |

Germany and France are the sharpest contrast: in Germany an email address satisfies the obligation, in France it cannot, because only an accredited platform may transmit. A single "EU answer" gets one of those two wrong by definition.

The small-business VAT exemption behaves differently in all four: Belgium does **not** exempt a founder from *issuing* structured e-invoices; Germany does **not** exempt them from *receiving*; the Netherlands has no mandate at all and its announced one reportedly **does** carve out KOR businesses.

Thresholds diverge just as much:

| | Small-business VAT threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Belgium | €25,000, rising €1,000/year from 2027 to €30,000 in 2031 — **enacted**, law of 2026-09-23 art. 26 |
| Netherlands | €20,000 domestic; €100,000 Union-wide for the EU-KOR, with a quarterly report |
| Germany | €25,000 previous year **and** €100,000 current year, both operative |
| France | €37,500 previous year / €41,250 current year for services; €85,000 / €93,500 for sales |

None of those is "the EU threshold". [eu-vat-sme.md](eu-vat-sme.md) records why: Directive (EU) 2020/285 art. 284(1) sets a **ceiling** of €85,000 on what a member state may choose, so France's sales figure sits exactly at the ceiling while the Netherlands chose a quarter of it. A founder asking for "the EU figure" is asking a question with no answer.

**One trap worth naming.** Germany's €100,000 is a **domestic, current-year** figure under §19 UStG. The directive's €100,000 is a **Union-wide, prior-year** cap for the cross-border exemption. Same number, unrelated rules — and a plausible place to be confidently wrong.

And the invoicing duty attached to the exemption inverts: a Dutch KOR user is **not required to issue an invoice at all**, while a Belgian founder under the exemption **must issue a structured electronic one**. Carrying either answer across a border produces a confidently wrong result.

Any skill that answers these from a single "EU" position is wrong in at least three countries. That is the reason packs exist.

### Four mechanisms for one idea — unfair terms between businesses

Every one of the four controls unreasonable standard terms between businesses. No two do it the same way, and the **remedy** differs, which is what actually matters to a founder deciding whether a clause is worth fighting.

| | Mechanism | Remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Belgium | General norm **plus** a statutory black list (irrebuttable) and grey list (rebuttable), art. VI.91/3–91/5 WER | The term is unfair |
| Germany | The §§308/309 catalogue is **disapplied** in B2B by §310(1) BGB, but the general §307 test survives | The term is ineffective |
| Netherlands | Open test in art. 6:233 BW; the black and grey lists are **consumer-only**, but `reflexwerking` can import them for a small party. Art. 6:235 **excludes large parties** from the protection entirely | The term is voidable |
| France | **Two routes.** Art. L442-1 I C.com catches imposing — or **attempting** to impose — a `déséquilibre significatif`. Art. 1171 C.civ catches a **non-negotiable** clause in an adhesion contract, excluding the principal object and price adequacy | L442-1: **damages**. Art. 1171: the clause is **deemed unwritten** |

Two traps for anyone reasoning across borders:

- **"B2B means the lists don't apply" is the German answer, and only the German one.** In Belgium the lists apply between businesses by design.
- **Dutch protection depends on the founder's own size.** Under 50 employees and not publishing accounts, a founder is protected by 6:233 and may draw on the consumer lists. Past that, art. 6:235 removes the protection — the same clause, the same counterparty, a different outcome.

### Where the four actually converge — software ownership

Not everything diverges, and it is worth recording when it does not. All four give **employee-created** software to the employer by default, all four let a contract say otherwise, and **none of them transfers anything for commissioned work.**

| | Employee-created software | Mechanism | Commissioned work |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Belgium | employer gets the economic rights | official guidance, `behoudens andersluidende overeenkomst` | only **usage rights** pass; transfer of ownership must be **explicit and in writing** |
| Netherlands | employer is **deemed the maker** | art. 7 Auteurswet, `fictief makerschap` | `overeenkomst van opdracht` does not qualify |
| Germany | employer alone exercises **all** economic rights | §69b UrhG, employment and service relationships | statute is **silent** — covers neither |
| France | rights **devolved** to the employer, incl. the documentation | art. L113-9 CPI | speaks only of `employés` |

The Dutch construction is the outlier in *mechanism*: the employer becomes the author, not merely the rights holder, so moral rights sit differently there.

**The consequence is the same in all four, and it matters for every founder using this skill.** A solo founder is a contractor, not their client's employee — so by default **they keep the rights** in what they build, and a client who wants them needs an express transfer. The mirror image also holds: a founder who *paid a freelancer* does not own the result merely by having paid.

This convergence is itself a verified finding, not an assumption. Each pack cites its own primary source; none borrows another's.

**What the client *does* get is a separate question, and all four now have an answer — but not to the same depth.** Three rest on primary legislation read per article; Belgium's rests on official guidance only, which the table says. Each reaches "write it down" by a different route, and the differences are drafting instructions, not trivia.

| | Rule on an unwritten or vague grant | Primary source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Germany | **Purpose**. Where the types of use are not named, the scope is whatever the **shared contract purpose** implies | §31(5) UrhG |
| Netherlands | **Form, then narrow construction**. A transfer needs writing **and a deed** (`akte`, art. 3:95 BW); an exclusive licence needs writing; a non-exclusive licence needs no form. What is granted by the maker covers **only** what is expressly stated or **necessarily** follows from the agreement's nature and purport — and that protection is disapplied for an art. 7 or art. 8 maker, so it follows the human creator | art. 2(3) and 2(5) Auteurswet |
| France | **Itemisation, plus a floor that cannot be removed**. Each right transferred needs a **distinct mention**, and the domain of exploitation must be bounded as to **extent, destination, place and duration**. Separately, the lawful user keeps a statutory floor — use for its purpose, a backup copy, study and testing, interoperability decompilation — and stipulations against the last three are **void** | art. L131-3 and L122-6-1 CPI |
| Belgium | **Usage rights, and nothing more.** Rights are *"niet automatisch overgedragen"* for commissioned software: by default only **usage rights** pass, the author keeps ownership, and a transfer of the economic rights must be **explicit and in writing**. Art. XI.296 WER confirms the employment rule reaches only `werknemers of beambten` | official guidance, plus art. XI.296 WER |

Two things worth lifting out:

- **The Netherlands is the only one with a form requirement for ownership.** No deed, no transfer — so a Dutch founder cannot lose the copyright by invoice and a Dutch client cannot gain it by payment.
- **Germany and the Netherlands read silence against the grantee; France reads it against the drafting.** Same practical instruction, different failure modes.

The instruction that follows is the same in all four regardless: **name the types of use in writing.** Silence favours neither side; it just moves the decision to a court.

### And a floor the contract cannot reach — with four different perimeters

All four implement the EU Software Directive's user exceptions, so a founder could reasonably assume this part is harmonised and skip it. **It is not harmonised in the one respect that decides a negotiation: which of these rights a contract can take away.** All four articles were read individually.

| | Use for its purpose, **incl. error correction** | Backup copy | Observe, study, test | Decompile for interoperability | Where the nullity rule sits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Belgium** | XI.299 §1 — **waivable** (`Tenzij bij overeenkomst uitdrukkelijk anders is bepaald`) | XI.299 §2 — **unwaivable** | XI.299 §3 — **unwaivable** | XI.300 — **unwaivable** | XI.301: XI.299 §§2–3 and XI.300 are `van dwingend recht` |
| **Germany** | §69d(1) — **waivable** (`soweit keine besonderen vertraglichen Bestimmungen vorliegen`) | §69d(2) — **unwaivable**, and the text says so twice | §69d(3) — **unwaivable** | §69e — **unwaivable** | §69g(2): terms contrary to §69d(2),(3),(5),(7) or §69e are `nichtig` |
| **France** | L122-6-1 I — **waivable** as to error correction, which the author may reserve | L122-6-1 II — **unwaivable** | L122-6-1 III — **unwaivable** | L122-6-1 IV — **unwaivable** | L122-6-1 VIII: stipulations contrary to II, III and IV are void |
| **Netherlands** | 45j — general rule **waivable**, but **loading, displaying and error correction cannot be contractually prohibited** | 45k — **no express nullity clause** | 45l — **no express nullity clause** | 45m — **no express nullity clause** | Only art. 45j, second sentence |

**The Netherlands inverts the pattern the other three share.** In Belgium, Germany and France, error correction is the one thing a contract *can* take away, and the backup copy, study right and decompilation are the things it cannot. In the Dutch text, error correction is expressly protected and the other three carry no express nullity provision at all.

**What a founder should do with that, stated carefully:**

- **The advice "your client can always fix bugs" is true in the Netherlands and false in the other three.** In Belgium, Germany and France, error correction is a term to negotiate on purpose. A client who depends on the software and cannot fix it has a commercial problem no statute will solve.
- **The advice "you cannot contract away a backup copy" is verified for Belgium, Germany and France** — a clause attempting it is void, not merely unusual.
- **For the Netherlands, this pack does not say those three are waivable.** The absence of an express nullity clause in arts. 45k–45m is a fact about the text; whether Dutch law treats them as mandatory by another route is **[not researched]**. That is a reason to get Dutch advice on such a clause, not a reason to assume either answer.

**Why this is in the README rather than stated once in a shared EU layer.** The shared layers exist for rules that are genuinely identical in all four — the GDPR register test, the VAT threshold ceiling. This one looked identical and is not. Writing it once would have erased the only part a founder needs: which of the four they are in.

### Same deadline, different machinery — notifying a data breach

Art. 33 GDPR gives every member state the same 72 hours. **How you actually file inside those 72 hours is national, undocumented in the Regulation, and different enough to cost a founder the deadline.** Two authorities have been read at this level of detail:

| | How it is filed | What follows the first filing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Belgium | **Two linked parts** on the GBA portal. Part 2 needs a business account, FAS authentication and the MijneGov role `GBA_Documentum_Vertegenwoordiger`. Part 1 can be filed with **no account**, using the KBO number | **21 calendar days** for part 2, reminders at 14 and 7 days. Cancellable until part 2 is filed, never after. Part 1 alone does **not** discharge the duty |
| Netherlands | **One online form, and only that form** — the AP accepts no notification by post or email. No account or role is needed | Mark it **provisional or definitive**. If provisional, the AP asks for a follow-up **within 4 weeks**; longer must be motivated. With no definitive notification you may not have met art. 33 |

Germany and France were **[not researched]** at this level — do not assume either resembles the two above.

What transfers from these two to any jurisdiction:

- **The deadline is not the only clock.** Both authorities expect a *completion* step weeks after the first filing, and in both it is the completion, not the first filing, that discharges art. 33. A founder who files inside 72 hours and stops is not finished.
- **Set the access up before you need it.** The Belgian route is the clearest case — the GBA itself warns the 72 hours can be lost to an unassigned role — but the general rule is to find out now what account, role or form your authority requires, and give more than one person the keys.
- **The register is the breach document.** Both authorities assume you can say *who is affected* on day one. Nothing else you have will tell you.

### Same article, four national lists — when is an impact assessment compulsory?

Art. 35(4) GDPR obliges **every** supervisory authority to publish a list of the processing that requires an impact assessment. Those lists are not summaries of the Regulation; they are additional, binding enumerations, and all four of these are now read in full.

| | Entries | An AI tool that converses with customers | The entry most likely to catch a one-person B2B business |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Germany — the DSK's joint list | 17 | **entry 11 names it**: AI used to steer interaction with data subjects or to evaluate them, with `Kundensupport mittels künstlicher Intelligenz` as its named field of use | that same entry |
| Netherlands — the AP's `Besluit` | 17 | not named. It can still need an assessment through the general rule or the innovation criterion — but that is an argument, not a list | **entry 15**, profiling, which drops art. 35(3)(a)'s requirement that decisions with legal effect follow |
| Belgium — GBA decision 01/2019 | **8** | not named | **entry 2**, third-party data used to decide to refuse or end a service contract — **and it carries no scale qualifier at all** |
| France — CNIL `Délibération 2018-327` | unnumbered | not named | **profiling for HR purposes**, whose own examples include recruitment assisted by a selection algorithm; and **profiling on externally sourced data**, whose examples include personalising online advertising |

So the same AI pilot is a listed case in Germany and an argument in the other three; a profiling feature art. 35(3)(a) would let past is listed in the Netherlands; a credit check before onboarding one client is listed in Belgium however small it is; and in France a recruitment-screening algorithm or an ad-personalisation model is listed on its own terms.

**And only France has published the other half of the article.** Art. 35(5) *permits* an authority to list processing for which no assessment is required, and the CNIL has: `Délibération 2019-118` exempts, among others, human-resources administration by organisations of **fewer than 250 persons** — expressly *excluding* any profiling — and supplier-relationship administration. The other three authorities, as read, publish the mandatory list only.

Belgium also commits to revisiting its list every six months, which none of the others does, so the four lists are not even equally stable.

What transfers: **"do I need an impact assessment" cannot be answered from the Regulation alone**, and the answer does not cross a border. Neither does the DPO question beside it — §38 BDSG compels a German one-person business to appoint one in cases where the Dutch `UAVG` and the Belgian `wet van 30 juli 2018` require nothing at all. Nor does the consequence of getting the lawful basis wrong: in Belgium art. 222 of that act makes processing without one a criminal offence carrying up to €120,000, where the other two leave it to the administrative fine.

## Using a pack

1. Establish the founder's country and region, the customers' country, and where the service is delivered. These can differ and each can matter.
2. If a pack matches, use it. Respect its `checked` date and its recorded conflicts.

   **Say so when a pack is stale.** If the pack is past its `Next full recheck due` date, or a claim's `recheck_trigger` has occurred, or its check date is older than roughly six months for anything consequential: still give the recorded figure, but tell the founder in the same breath that it is past its recheck date and needs confirming against the primary source before they rely on it. Recheck it yourself if the host can fetch. A stale pack that reads as authoritative is worse than no pack at all — never present its figures unqualified, and never quietly replace them with a figure from memory.
3. Record what you used in the project's `sources` with the pack's URL and its check date — not with today's date, unless you actually refetched the page yourself.

## When no pack matches

Do not generalize from Belgium or from any other pack. Rules that look similar between neighbouring countries routinely differ in threshold, start date and scope. Instead:

1. Work from the universal topic list in [../pilot-conditions.md](../pilot-conditions.md). The **topics** are jurisdiction-neutral; only the answers are local.
2. For each topic the pilot actually depends on, identify the responsible official body for that jurisdiction (tax authority, business register, data-protection authority, economic affairs ministry) and check its own current page. Prefer the official body over an accountancy blog, a vendor page or a news article.
3. Record each claim using the `sources` shape in [../dossier-format.md](../dossier-format.md): URL, source type, what it covers, published/effective dates where stated, your check date, and a recheck trigger.
4. State plainly what you could not verify. An unchecked topic stays unresolved and keeps blocking the condition it blocks.
5. Never produce a number — a threshold, rate, deadline or fee — that you did not read in a source you actually fetched in this session or that is not recorded in a pack. If asked for one you cannot verify, say so and name the body that publishes it.

## The pack template

Three kinds of file live here, distinguished by name:

- **`eu-*.md` — a shared legal layer.** A rule that is identical across member states, stated once instead of four times. These have their own shape.
- **`<country>.md` — a national pack.** These must follow the six-section template below, so a reader finds the same topic in the same place in every country.
- **`<pack>-<topic>.md` — an annex to that pack**, for one section that outgrew the per-reference budget. [germany-e-invoicing.md](germany-e-invoicing.md) was the first; [eu-gdpr-fines.md](eu-gdpr-fines.md) and [eu-gdpr-dpo-dpia.md](eu-gdpr-dpo-dpia.md) show that a shared layer can have them too. An annex is exempt from the template, because it covers one topic rather than six, but it still carries the convention line, a maintenance table and a recheck date, and **its pack must link it** — otherwise a reader working through the pack never learns it exists.

`scripts/check-skill.mjs` enforces all three rules. If you reorder or rename a section the build fails and names the section; if an annex is unlinked from its pack it says so, and it finds the pack from the filename, so there is no list to keep in sync. The parent is the **longest** prefix of the annex's own name that is itself a pack, so `eu-gdpr-fines.md` belongs to `eu-gdpr.md` and not to an `eu.md` that does not exist — and `eu-vat-sme.md`, which has no pack prefix at all, stays a layer.

**Write an annex only when a pack is over budget.** Splitting on taste instead costs the reader a second file to open for no gain. The check reports every file's size against the budget, so the moment to split is the moment it fails.

A consequence to expect: because the trigger is size and not subject, **the same topic can sit in the pack in one country and in an annex in another.** It happens to be symmetric today — all four national packs have a data-protection annex — but that is the result of four packs each crossing 48 KB, not of a decision that the topic belongs in an annex. The next country to be researched will keep its data protection in the pack until it does not fit. So always start from the pack's own section, which keeps the answer, and follow its pointer for the working.

```
# <Country> — jurisdiction pack

Scope: ...                       who this pack is for
Out of scope: ...                what needs separate research
**This pack is preparation, not advice.**
Verification convention: ...     copy it verbatim from any existing pack

## 1. Registration and formalities
## 2. VAT — <the national scheme's own name>
## 3. B2B e-invoicing — <its current status>
## 4. Agreements and unfair terms
## 5. Ownership and permitted use
## 6. Data protection             national authority; link the shared EU layers

## Pack maintenance
| Claim | Check date | Status |   one row per substantive claim
Next full recheck due: **YYYY-MM-DD**.
<a line stating no independent reviewer has reviewed this pack>
```

Sections 2 and 3 take the local name and status after the dash. Sections 1, 4, 5 and 6 are fixed. Within a section, use the structure the existing packs use: a source line with its type and check date, then a table where there are distinct cases, then the practical consequence for a founder, then what remains open.

### The Status column is a fixed vocabulary

Every row's Status cell **begins with one of these seven labels**, optionally followed by ` — ` and free prose. Nothing else is allowed, and `scripts/check-freshness.mjs` fails on an unknown label.

| Label | What it asserts | What a maintainer does with it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Primary` | the authoritative text itself was read — statute, directive, judgment, bill | nothing until the text is amended |
| `Authority` | an official regulator, tax-authority or chamber publication was read; the statute behind it was not | recheck when the authority republishes; upgrade to `Primary` where the claim is consequential |
| `Secondary` | only commentary, news, or a third-party reproduction was read | upgrade — this is the weakest basis a claim may rest on |
| `Unconfirmed` | the pack states something its sources do not establish — a source was checked and was silent or contradicted it, a fetch failed, or the claim rests on inference | either find the real basis or remove the claim. Never leave a consequential one here |
| `Withdrawn` | the claim was wrong and has been corrected in the pack | keep the row. It is the record of the correction |
| `Open` | this pack does not answer it | research it, or accept it as out of reach and say so in the detail |
| `Elsewhere` | answered in a shared `eu-*.md` layer rather than here | nothing; maintain it in that layer |

Three things this buys, which 90 hand-written phrasings did not:

- **It is scannable.** A maintainer picking up a pack reads one column and knows what to do, instead of parsing a different sentence per row.
- **It is countable.** `Open` is the row shape `check-freshness.mjs` compares against the `[not researched]` markers in the body, so the two cannot drift apart unnoticed.
- **Bold means something again.** The label is plain text. Reserve bold inside the detail for a warning a reader must not miss — `**Do not state the figures as law**`.

Put everything specific in the detail: which article, which letter, whether the pack quotes the source verbatim, what was *not* read. `Primary — §19 UStG, quoted` and `Authority — BMF letter of 2025-10-15, 33 pages extracted` both say more than the label alone, and both still scan.

`Open` is the one label whose detail is close to mandatory, because "no answer" is not useful on its own. Say why it is open: not yet researched, researched but inconclusive, or deliberately left to a professional.

### "Primary" means authoritative, not official

The first three labels are a judgment about **what kind of thing the source is**, and the inline `· *source type* ·` note on each claim in the body must agree with it. The distinction that keeps getting lost:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary** | the text that *is* the rule — a statute, a directive, a published judgment, a bill. Nothing else is primary, however official its publisher |
| **Authority** | a publication by the body that issues or supervises the rule: a tax authority's website, a supervisory authority's manual, a ministry's circular, a chamber's guidance, a government FAQ |
| **Secondary** | everything else, including a verbatim reproduction of a statute on a third-party host |

**A government website is `Authority`, not `Primary`.** This was wrong in three packs: a Belgian e-invoicing FAQ and three Belastingdienst pages were labelled *primary official*, which reads as though the statute had been read when it had not. "Official" describes who published it; "primary" describes whether it is the rule itself. A ministry can publish a perfectly official document that is nonetheless one step away from the law — a German `BMF-Schreiben` binds the tax administration, not the courts, so it is `Authority` even though the ministry wrote it and the pack read the letter itself.

**A verbatim reproduction is `Secondary`.** `wetboek.org` carries accurate text and `netherlands.md` used it until 2026-11-11. But if the reproduction is wrong, the claim is wrong, so the evidence is only as good as the host. Name the host in the detail and say what the authoritative source would be, so the row reads as an upgrade waiting to happen rather than as a finished one.

**And the risk is not only that a reproduction is wrong — it is that it is old.** `eu-ai-act.md` rested three articles on a per-article reproduction and so had no way to learn that Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 had replaced one of them. `eu-gdpr.md` cited eight articles the same way until 2026-11-11, when all eight were read on EUR-Lex instead: the GDPR turned out to be unamended, so nothing was stale, but reading it moved eighteen rows to `Primary` and turned up four claims the reproduction's wording or formatting had bent. **When the authoritative host is accepted and reachable, a `Secondary` row for a statute is a choice, not a constraint.** The accepted hosts are in `scripts/check-skill.mjs`, `AUTHORITATIVE_TEXT_HOSTS`.

The Dutch civil-code rows were the last of that kind, and they were held back by a tooling limit rather than a judgment: `wetten.overheid.nl` serves each Book of the Civil Code as one document — Boek 6 is 351,743 characters and Boek 7 over a million — and a plain fetch of it returns the wrong part. **Loading the page in a browser and slicing it to the article inside the page solves it**, and reading art. 6:235 that way immediately falsified one of the pack's claims. If a row says a statute could not be reached, try that before accepting it.

**And when a route works, check what version it gives you.** `ejustice.just.fgov.be` serves only metadata on its Justel pages, while its `article_body.pl` route — publication date plus `numac` — serves the full text **as published**. For a 1969 code amended monthly, or a 2014 decree whose threshold has since changed, that is the wrong version and worse than nothing: `KB nr. 19` as published reads €15,000 where the current figure is €25,000. A route that returns text is not the same as a route that returns the law in force.

Where the official source exists and is reachable, use it. Where it exists and is not — `wetten.overheid.nl` serves each Book of the Civil Code as one document of a quarter of a million characters — say that in the detail, so the next maintainer does not repeat the attempt blind.

## Contributing a pack

A pack is only worth having if it is verifiable. Requirements:

- One file per jurisdiction, named for the country in English, lowercase (`netherlands.md`, `germany.md`).
- Every substantive claim carries: the official source URL, the source's own publication or effective date where stated, the date you checked it, and a recheck trigger.
- Record conflicts between sources rather than choosing a winner silently. A preserved conflict is more useful than a confident wrong answer — see the basic-management entry in the Belgium pack for the pattern.
- Mark the boundary of each claim: who it applies to, from when, and what it does not cover.
- Never copy substantial source text. Summarize in original words and link. Where copying is unavoidable, record the licence basis.
- Add the pack to the table above with its check date and coverage.
- A pack is preparation, not qualified advice. Say so in the pack.

### Cold-read a pack you have edited more than once

Every check in `scripts/` tests a file against a rule. None of them reads it as a reader. That gap has a predictable shape, and on 2026-11-11 it produced ten defects across three files in one sitting:

- `netherlands.md` §4 ended with **"the BW articles were not read in primary form"**, four paragraphs below a source line saying they had been, that day, with a note explaining how.
- Its unfair-terms table answered *"Can a contract remove it?"* with **"the text carries no express nullity clause"** — true about the Dutch text and the wrong answer to the question, since a directive voids such clauses. A reader scanning the table took away "yes".
- `eu-gdpr.md` opened with **"each national pack carries only what is national: which supervisory authority is competent"**, after the national packs had acquired a DPO duty, four disagreeing impact-assessment lists, two ages of consent and criminal penalties in two countries. That is the sentence deciding whether the national pack is opened at all.
- `eu-ai-act.md` §1 routed a hiring tool **"outside this pack"** two sections before the pack acquired the article that governs it.

Every one of those sentences was true when written, is defensible in isolation, and passes every structural rule. They break because **a correction lands in one place and the sentence framing it stays where it is.**

So: **a pack edited more than once in a sitting gets read end to end before the sitting ends.** Not diffed — read. What to look for, in rough order of damage:

| Look for | Because |
| --- | --- |
| The **opening** paragraph of the file and of each section | it is what a reader acts on, and it is the furthest from where you were editing |
| Any **`[not researched]`** note near something you researched | the two drift apart silently and contradict each other |
| **Table cells** that answer something other than their column header | a table is scanned, not read, so a wrong cell travels further than a wrong sentence |
| Sentences saying a topic is **"outside this pack"** or **"open"** | those are the ones a new section makes false |
| The same fact stated **twice** in consecutive paragraphs | one of them arrived with an edit and neither was removed |

A cold read of a 45 KB pack takes a few minutes and has so far found defects at a rate nothing else matches.

## Review cadence

Packs rot. Thresholds move, obligations phase in, portals move URLs. A pack with a stale check date is a liability, because it reads as authoritative.

- Recheck anything with a scheduled change before its next step takes effect.
- Recheck the whole pack at least every six months and update its check date even when nothing changed — "checked and unchanged on <date>" is itself information.
- When a source URL dies, record the dead URL and the date it failed. Do not quietly replace it with a different page and keep the old date.
